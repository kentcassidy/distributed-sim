MVP Sprint Plan

v0.4.0 — thirteen days to a presentable minimum · Sep 1 – Sep 13, 2026

Rescopes the v0.3.0 charter to the real deadline. The charter still holds as the full vision; this is what actually gets built first. Partition invariance is the new V&V spine.

Ship one thing, complete: a working HLA federation, in C++, where two aircraft each own their own physics, one holds formation on its ghost of the other, and the result is shown to be invariant to how the work is split across federates at zero impairment — then to degrade predictably as network delay rises. A running federation with a real V&V result and a clean, documented repo. Nothing more, on time, beats anything more, late.

What carries the argument

The panel doubted two things: C++ depth and distributed-simulation experience. Every hour goes to the smallest artifact that rebuts both. That artifact is a federation that runs, in C++, and produces a result with a number on it. The result is partition invariance; the legibility is the repo. Those two are load-bearing. Everything else waits.

Load-bearing — build first

C++ model core, entity-agnostic (owns a list of aircraft, partitioned by config) · linearized longitudinal model, verified against published modes · two aircraft + logger on Portico · hand-authored FOM · conservative time management · one dead-reckoning algo (constant velocity, fixed threshold) · formation coupling on the ghost · partition-invariance V&V · one latency sweep · public repo, ~6–7 ADRs, journal, 2-page V&V writeup.

Cut to post-9/13

Storm + Dryden turbulence · Monte Carlo ensemble · distribution-drift comparison · batch and replay drivers · netem (use an in-federate delay buffer instead) · second DR algorithm · DR-threshold and lookahead sweeps · VM transparency run · MCMC · lateral-directional / tabulated aero · four-aircraft fractional ratios.

Why the cuts are safe A running federation with a latency result rebuts both doubts completely. An unfinished ensemble rebuts neither. The cut list is entirely enrichment on top of an already-complete argument — and every item on it is one you can add after the 13th, in front of an audience, as “here's where it's going next.”
Cut fluff, never legibility The documentation is not fluff — it is the half of the rebuttal the panel couldn't see last time. A bare pile of working code answers “can he do distributed sim” and completely fails “does he work in a way we can trust.” Trim ADRs to the six or seven that matter, keep journal entries to two lines, hold the V&V report to two pages — but the clean, decision-documented public repo stays. Cut the physics ambition, not the trail of reasoning.

The V&V spine: partition invariance

The validity claim is that the answer must not depend on how the work is divided across federates. It is the federation analog of numerical reproducibility, and it validates the distribution itself — not just the physics.

Same scenario, same physics, two ways of splitting the work. The comparison is the V&V.

The two claims, in order

  1. Invariance (the validation). Run the identical deterministic scenario with both aircraft co-located on one federate, then split one to each. At zero injected delay and zero DR error, the aggregate trajectories must match within a tight tolerance. A match proves the distribution added nothing — the federated answer equals the single-machine answer.
  2. Controlled breakdown (the result). Now sweep injected delay in the split case. The follower's station-keeping degrades while the co-located case stays put. The gap between them, plotted against delay, is the measured cost of putting the network inside the control loop — and it can be cross-checked against the analytical delay-margin prediction.
Why this idea fits the moment Cutting the ensemble left the MVP without a headline result. Partition invariance is a headline result, and it needs no ensemble, no storm, no hundreds of runs — only the federation you were already building, run twice and compared. It replaces the cut ensemble as the V&V spine and doubles as the latency result. This is the strongest single addition available inside 13 days.
Requirement, and the ratio caveat Exact invariance holds only for deterministic runs — no turbulence, conservative time management — so comparison is meaningful. (Another reason the storm stays cut for the MVP: its randomness would break bit-comparison.) Also: with strict entity ownership the divisible unit is the entity, so two aircraft give “co-located vs split,” not literal 25/75. True fractional ratios need a four-aircraft formation chain — a clean stretch goal, kept off the MVP path. Build the core entity-agnostic now (owns a list, partition by config) so the four-aircraft version is later a config change, not a rewrite.

Sequenced by dependency. Portico bring-up and time management are the two places days vanish, so both have a checkpoint and a fallback. Documentation runs continuously, not as a final phase.

Checkpoints and fallbacks — decide at these, don't drift past them

End of Sep 4 — if two federates aren't exchanging an attribute yet, you're behind on the hardest infrastructure. Stop adding scope; get the handshake working before anything else.
End of Sep 9 — if conservative time management isn't working, fall back to best-effort receive-order, ship the federation without conservative time, and say so plainly in the limitations. A running federation with honest limits beats a stalled one reaching for correctness. Time management is the highest-value piece, so fight for it — but not past this line.
End of Sep 11 — you must have at least the co-located-vs-split match plus one delay point. That is the minimum shippable result. Everything after is refinement of a result that already exists.
The one failure mode to avoid Being 70% into the full v0.3.0 vision on Sep 13 with nothing that runs end to end. That is the only outcome worse than a small complete thing. Protect completeness over ambition at every fork: a federation that runs, validates, and is documented — even if it's two aircraft and an in-code delay buffer — is a win. Reach for the storm and the ensemble after that exists, not before.

On the deadline framing itself

A dropped-off project reopens a conversation; it is not itself a selection, and dropping it uninvited can read either as impressive or as not taking the no. The lightest touch is best — a short, warm note to the flight chief that you built it because the gap he named was fair, with a link, and no ask attached. Let the work carry it. That means the work has to be finished by the 13th, which is the entire reason for cutting to the MVP. Ship complete, then say “here's where it goes next” — and the cut list is exactly that roadmap.
